# Figure captions

Each caption states what is plotted, for which site and scenario, in which
units, and which results file it is drawn from. Figures are regenerated by
`python src/pipeline.py figures` into `results/figures/` (300 dpi PNG and
PDF). Colours are colour-blind-safe; every series is also identified by
marker, line style or a direct label, so the figures read in greyscale.
Figure numbers are those of the paper.

**Figure 1. Model structure (`fig1_model_structure`).** Household demand
(appliance loads from survey ownership plus separate cooking meals) feeds an
hourly simulation of solar and battery operation over a 20-year project. The
sizing search runs it for each candidate design and keeps the cheapest
two-stage system that leaves at most 5% of demand unserved in every year. The
finance model returns the levelised cost, the net present value and the
operating subsidy under each tariff. Dashed arrows show external data.

**Figure 2. Cost and price per kWh by share of households cooking
(`fig_cost_vs_price`).** (a) LCOE (US$/kWh, real, 12% discount rate) at 0, 50
and 100% electric cooking, split into per-connection costs (the connection,
its share of soft costs and the customer part of operating cost), which do not
change with use, and the cost of solar, battery and inverter with their soft
costs, second stage, replacement and upkeep. (b) Average price paid per kWh
under the national tariffs, weighted by present value; labels give the LCOE as
a multiple of that price. 300 households, design weather year. Source:
`results/cost_vs_price.csv`.

**Figure 3. Average price per unit by monthly household use
(`fig5_blended_tariff`).** US$/kWh under Rwanda's block tariff (RURA,
effective 1 October 2025) and Kenya's single-band tariff (EPRA 2023, rates
from 1 July 2025), converted at RWF 1,460 and KES 129 per US$. Open markers
show the simulated average household without cooking and filled markers with
all meals cooked, in the first year. Source:
`results/tariff_band_recomputed.csv`.

**Figure 4. Moving supper cooking to the afternoon (`fig_daytime`).** All
households cooking; share φ of supper cooking moved, 0.1 to 0.5. Lines are
means of 24 seeds and bands the 95% interval of the mean, which reflects
sampling noise only. (a) Batteries bought over the project: saving against the
flat tariff, %. (b) Largest daytime discount (US cents/kWh) the operator can
give before the saving is used up. (c) Change in the cooking peak, %. Source:
`results/ensemble_summary.csv`.

**Figure 5. Sensitivity to inputs (`fig_sensitivity`).** Each bar spans the
result between the low and the high value of one input, with the system
re-sized each time; the top pair moves capital cost, operating cost, the
discount rate and collection together. (a) LCOE with all households cooking,
US$/kWh. (b) Extra operating subsidy per cooking household at the national
tariff, US$ per year. Vertical lines mark the base case. Source:
`results/sensitivity.csv`.

**Not in the paper. Modelled cooking load by hour of day
(`fig2_cooking_profile`).** Mean electric cooking load per cooking household
(W), 300 households, 1 kW electric pressure cooker, ten seeds, no shifting.
Shaded bands are the mealtime windows seen in metered stove data (Scott et
al., 2025). The spread of start times is fitted to the Kenyan diversity factor
(9.75) and the hour of the peak follows from the meal times chosen, so neither
validates the model; the daily energy (1.00 kWh against 1.30 kWh measured) is
an independent check. Source: `results/cooking_validation_summary.csv`.
